# Vitamin D fact sheet for health professionals

- Publisher: NIH Office of Dietary Supplements
- Updated: 2025-06-27
- Source type: official nutrient status, dosing, safety, and interaction overview
- Checked: 2026-05-03
- Ingested: 2026-05-03

## Summary

ODS describes vitamin D as a fat-soluble vitamin/hormone precursor obtained from sun exposure, foods, and supplements. Vitamin D is biologically inert until hydroxylated to 25(OH)D and then 1,25(OH)2D. Serum 25(OH)D is the main status marker, but thresholds and assay variability complicate interpretation. The Food and Nutrition Board considers 50 nmol/L (20 ng/mL) or more adequate for most people, 30 to under 50 nmol/L inadequate for bone and overall health, and below 30 nmol/L associated with deficiency. Levels above 125 nmol/L can be linked to adverse effects.

## Safety Notes

- Adult recommended intakes are generally 600-800 IU/day depending on age, while the adult tolerable upper intake level is 4,000 IU/day.
- Toxicity can cause hypercalcemia, hypercalciuria, nausea, vomiting, weakness, neuropsychiatric disturbances, dehydration, polyuria, excessive thirst, kidney stones, renal failure, soft-tissue calcification, arrhythmias, and death.
- Toxicity is usually from excessive supplement intake rather than sun exposure.
- Vitamin D supplements may interact with medications or be affected by medications including orlistat, statins, steroids, and thiazide diuretics.
- Baseline 25(OH)D status and calcium context matter before interpreting benefits or harms.
