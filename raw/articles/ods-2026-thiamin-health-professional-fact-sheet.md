# NIH ODS thiamin health professional fact sheet

NIH Office of Dietary Supplements health-professional fact sheet for thiamin / vitamin B1, captured as a safety, status, and product-identity anchor for thiamine-derivative cognition claims.

Key details captured 2026-05-03:

- Thiamin is vitamin B1, a water-soluble vitamin naturally present in some foods, added to some foods, and available as a supplement.
- It plays a critical role in energy metabolism, growth, development, and cell function.
- Humans store only small amounts, so continuous intake is required.
- Thiamin diphosphate / pyrophosphate is the main metabolically active form and is a cofactor for enzymes involved in glucose, amino acid, and lipid metabolism.
- Blood thiamin levels are not reliable status indicators; erythrocyte transketolase activity and urinary excretion are discussed as indirect measures.
- The adult RDA is 1.2 mg/day for men and 1.1 mg/day for women, with higher pregnancy/lactation needs.
- Common supplement forms include thiamin mononitrate and thiamin hydrochloride.
- Benfotiamine is described as a synthetic thiamin derivative used in some supplements, not water soluble, and converted to thiamin in the body.
- Deficiency can cause confusion, short-term memory loss, muscle weakness, cardiovascular symptoms, beriberi, Wernicke's encephalopathy, and Korsakoff's psychosis.
- Risk groups include people with alcohol dependence, older adults, people with HIV/AIDS, people with diabetes, and people after bariatric surgery.
- No tolerable upper intake level has been set because of lack of reports of adverse effects from high oral intakes, but lack of a UL is not proof of unlimited safety or no clinical monitoring need.

Wiki interpretation:

- Thiamine-derivative cognition anecdotes should be routed through status, deficiency, alcohol use, diabetes, bariatric surgery, malabsorption, and medication context before being treated as nootropic effects.
- This source supports a biologically plausible energy-metabolism and deficiency-correction frame, but does not establish benfotiamine, sulbutiamine, or TTFD as healthy-adult cognitive enhancers.
