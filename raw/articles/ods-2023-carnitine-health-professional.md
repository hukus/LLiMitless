# NIH ODS Carnitine Health Professional Fact Sheet

- Source: NIH Office of Dietary Supplements.
- Page updated: 2023-04-17.
- Ingested: 2026-05-03.

## Summary

ODS describes carnitine as a generic term covering L-carnitine, acetyl-L-carnitine, and propionyl-L-carnitine. Carnitine is made endogenously from lysine and methionine and is not essential for healthy people under ordinary conditions. It transports long-chain fatty acids into mitochondria for energy production and transports some toxic compounds out of mitochondria.

The fact sheet states that acetyl-L-carnitine bioavailability is not well studied, while supplemental L-carnitine absorption is lower than dietary L-carnitine. Carnitine supplements commonly range from about 3 mg to 5,000 mg. There is no established tolerable upper intake level, but doses around 3 g/day can cause nausea, vomiting, abdominal cramps, diarrhea, and fishy body odor. ODS also notes possible muscle weakness in people with uremia, seizure risk in people with seizure disorders, and unresolved TMAO / cardiovascular-risk questions from gut-microbiome metabolism.

## Interpretation

Use this as the safety and nutrient-status anchor. Healthy people generally synthesize enough carnitine, and high-dose supplement use should be interpreted through GI tolerability, seizure/renal vulnerability, and unresolved TMAO risk rather than through cognition claims alone.
